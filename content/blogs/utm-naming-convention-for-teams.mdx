A UTM link is only as useful as the spelling on it. If three people tag the same campaign three ways, your reports show three campaigns. A naming convention is the cheap fix: a page of rules everyone follows, and a way to notice when someone does not.

Here is a convention small enough to remember, the free template to start from, and how to enforce it without buying anything.

## The six rules

1. **Lowercase everything.** GA4 treats `Email` and `email` as different values.
2. **One separator between words.** A hyphen or an underscore. Never both, and never spaces.
3. **Source is the platform.** `google`, `linkedin`, `newsletter`. Use the full name, not `fb` or `li`.
4. **Medium is the type of traffic**, from a short approved list: `cpc`, `paid-social`, `email`, `social`, `referral`. These are values GA4's default channel grouping recognises, which keeps links out of "Unassigned". Check the list against [Google's default channel group](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9756891) before you freeze it.
5. **Campaign names follow one pattern.** For example `name-year-month`: `spring-sale-2026-11`. Two people naming the same campaign then arrive at the same text.
6. **Never tag links inside your own site.** A tagged internal link can overwrite where the visit really came from.

## Approved channels: the list that does the work

The rules above are easy to agree to and easy to break. The part that actually keeps data clean is a short list of approved source and medium pairs, one per channel your team uses:

```
Google Ads, google, cpc
LinkedIn Ads, linkedin, paid-social
Newsletter, newsletter, email
Organic social, linkedin, social
Partner posts, acme, referral
```

Anything not on the list is a conversation, not a new value typed in a hurry. Give the list one owner. When a new channel appears, the owner adds it and the version changes, so you always know which rules a link followed.

## A log nobody has to maintain by hand

A convention without a record turns into folklore. For each link, keep four things: the link, who made it, when, and which version of the rules it followed. That is enough to answer "where did this tag come from?" six months later.

The [UTM builder](/lab/utm-builder) writes these into its exports: the CSV and the Google Sheets copy end with the GA4 channel, the date created, the owner and the rules version. You can also download a [free template](/lab/utm-builder) with the same columns and three example rows; it is the button under "A naming convention you can copy" on that page.

## Enforce it without paid software

Paid UTM tools sell enforcement: dropdowns instead of free text, approved lists, roles. You can get most of that for free if you accept two limits: it runs in the browser, and it lives on each person's device.

- **Rules as a link.** In the builder, open "Working in a team? Set naming rules", paste your approved channels, pick the separator and optionally a campaign pattern such as `{name}-{yyyy}-{mm}`. Copy the rules link and send it to your team. Anyone who opens it gets a form that offers only those channels and writes names the agreed way. Nothing is stored on a server.
- **Flags, not blocks.** Names that do not fit the pattern are flagged in the saved list, so a one-off exception stays possible and visible.
- **Check old links.** Paste existing links into the [link checker](/lab/utm-link-checker). It flags capital letters, spaces, misspelled parameter names and combinations GA4 files under Unassigned, and builds a cleaned link.
- **Test before launch.** The campaign pack download includes a checklist: open each link, confirm the parameters survive redirects, watch GA4's Realtime report, scan each QR code.

## When the damage is already done

If reports are already split, merge them in a copy of the report. The [report cleaner](/lab/utm-report-cleaner) finds rows that are the same campaign written differently and gives you the mapping; the [guide to fixing split campaign names](/blog/ga4-campaign-names-split-by-capital-letters) walks through it.

## What this does not solve

A convention cannot fix links that lose their parameters in a redirect, consent settings that drop attribution, or an ad platform that overrides your tags. It makes your own tagging consistent, which is the part you control.
